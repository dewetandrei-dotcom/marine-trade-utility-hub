The Ultimate Fiberglass Boat Repair Guide

Fiberglass (FRP) boats are incredibly durable, but they are not indestructible. Whether you've hit a dock, grounded on a reef, or simply have an old boat with stress cracks, learning how to repair fiberglass is an essential skill for any boat owner.

Polyester vs. Epoxy: Which should you use?

This is the most common question DIY boat restorers ask. The answer depends on your repair type and budget.

Epoxy Resin

  • Pros: Much stronger than polyester. It is waterproof (polyester is slightly porous). It bonds mechanically and chemically to almost any material, including old fiberglass, wood, and metal. It has a 50:50 mix ratio (usually), making it easy to measure.
  • Cons: Expensive. It requires careful mixing. It takes longer to cure in cold weather.
  • Best for: Structural repairs, below-waterline repairs, and bonding to old fiberglass.

Polyester Resin

  • Pros: Cheap. Cures very quickly. It is what your boat is likely made of originally.
  • Cons: Brittle. Does not bond well to cured epoxy or other materials. Requires MEKP catalyst (which is toxic and tricky to dose). It can shrink over time and is slightly porous.
  • Best for: Cosmetic repairs above the waterline, and when building a brand new part from scratch on a mold.

Rule of thumb: Never use polyester over cured epoxy. You can use epoxy over cured polyester (as long as it is properly ground).

Will new fiberglass stick to old fiberglass?

Yes, new fiberglass will stick to old fiberglass, provided the old surface is properly prepped. If you lay new resin or glass directly onto old, shiny fiberglass, it will not bond and will eventually delaminate (peel off).

How to Make New Fiberglass Stick:

  1. Grind the surface: Use a coarse-grit sandpaper (16, 24, or 36-grit) on a grinder or sander to remove all the shine, paint, or gelcoat down to the fresh, rough laminate beneath. You should see the glass fibers.
  2. Bevel the edges: If you are filling a hole, you must "taper" or bevel the edges outward so that the new fiberglass overlaps smoothly. You want a 12:1 ratio (for every 1 unit of thickness, taper out 12 units).
  3. Clean thoroughly: Blow away all dust with compressed air, then wipe the area down with acetone or styrene to remove grease, wax, and oils. Do this immediately before applying the resin.

What is the best filler for fiberglass boats?

Choosing the right filler depends on where the repair is and what final finish you want.

  • Structural Fillers: Use a mixture of epoxy resin and high-density fillers (like colloidal silica or milled fibers). This creates a strong, waterproof paste for filling gouges, voids, or bonding hardware.
  • Fairing Compounds: Use a mixture of epoxy and low-density microballoons. This creates a lightweight, easy-to-sand paste used for smoothing out the final surface before painting or gelcoating.
  • West System Fillers: Products like 406 (Colloidal Silica) and 407 (Low-Density Filler) are the industry standards for these applications.

How much does it cost to fix fiberglass on a boat?

Professional marine labor rates generally run from $75 to $150+ per hour. A small, light gelcoat scratch might cost $75 to $200 to fix professionally. A major hole or structural repair can easily cost $2,000 to $10,000+.

By doing the repair yourself, you only pay for materials. A DIY repair of a 1-square-foot area using epoxy and cloth typically costs less than $50 in materials. You can use our calculator above to get a precise estimate for your specific repair.

Step-by-Step Overview of the Repair Process

  1. Preparation: Grind and bevel the damaged area to create a smooth slope and taper. Clean the surface thoroughly with acetone.
  2. Cut the Glass: Cut your fiberglass cloth or mat to the shape of the repair. Cut multiple layers, with each layer slightly larger than the last.
  3. Wet Out: Apply a layer of mixed resin to the prepared surface. Lay down your first piece of glass and press it into the resin.
  4. Layering: Apply more resin and add subsequent layers of glass until you have built up the thickness to match the original laminate.
  5. Fairing: Once the repair is fully cured, sand it smooth. Apply a fairing compound to fill any low spots, and sand again.
  6. Finishing: Apply gelcoat or marine paint to match the surrounding area.
